**Notation ETS actually uses in Part 3** (needed for the site to stay faithful):
- A segment and its length are both written in plain italic letters: $AB$. ETS does **not** use $\overline{AB}$ anywhere in Part 3 (p. 92).
- Angle: the word "angle" or the symbol: $\angle APC$ (p. 93). ETS writes "the measure of angle $A$ is $50^\circ$". It does **not** use $m\angle ABC$.
- Perpendicular: $k \perp m$ (p. 93). Parallel: $k \parallel m$ (p. 94; it is printed as `k || m`).
- ETS never uses the symbols $\cong$ or $\sim$ in Part 3. It writes the words "congruent" and "similar" (pp. 101–102).
- Degrees: the angle labels in figures are written $x^\circ$, $50^\circ$, and so on. Formulas use $180^\circ$ and $360^\circ$.
- Triangles and polygons are named by their vertex letters: "triangle $ABC$", "rectangle $ABCD$", "parallelogram $PQRS$".
- Arcs are named by **three** points: "arc $ABC$" (p. 107). ETS does not use the $\overset{\frown}{AC}$ symbol.

### Facts / formulas
- Opposite (vertical) angles have equal measure, so opposite angles are congruent (p. 93).
- The four angles formed by two intersecting lines sum to $360^\circ$ (p. 93).
- Perpendicular lines: each of the four angles measures $90^\circ$ (p. 93).
- Two parallel lines cut by a third line form eight angles. Four of them have measure $x^\circ$ and the other four have measure $y^\circ$, where $x + y = 180$ (p. 95).

### Gotchas
- $AB$ can mean the segment or its length; the context decides (p. 92). The Conventions add that it can also mean the line through $A$ and $B$ (see below).
- A right angle is shown by a small square at the vertex (p. 94).

### Figures
- **Geometry Figure 6 (p. 95):** three convex shapes with captions underneath: "Triangle (3 sides)", "Quadrilateral (4 sides)", "Pentagon (5 sides)". The triangle is scalene and obtuse, and the quadrilateral is irregular. There are no vertex labels.
- **Geometry Figure 7 (p. 96):** on the left, an irregular quadrilateral split into 2 triangles by one diagonal. On the right, an irregular pentagon split into 3 triangles by 2 segments from one vertex (the upper-right vertex) to the two nonadjacent vertices. There are no labels.

### Facts / formulas
- A polygon with $n$ sides can be divided into $n - 2$ triangles (p. 96).
- Sum of interior angles of an $n$-sided polygon: $(n-2)(180^\circ)$ (p. 96).
  - Quadrilateral: $(4-2)(180^\circ) = 360^\circ$. Hexagon: $(6-2)(180^\circ) = 720^\circ$ (p. 96).
  - Octagon: $(8-2)(180^\circ) = 1{,}080^\circ$. In a regular octagon each angle is $\dfrac{1{,}080^\circ}{8} = 135^\circ$ (p. 96).
- Each interior angle of a regular $n$-gon is $\dfrac{(n-2)(180^\circ)}{n}$. ETS shows this only through the octagon example; the general formula is our note, not ETS.

### Gotchas
- "Polygon" always means a **convex** polygon here: every interior angle is less than $180^\circ$ (p. 95).
- "Area of a polygon" means the area of the **region enclosed** by it (p. 96).

### Facts / formulas
- The interior angles of a triangle sum to $180^\circ$ (p. 96).
- **Triangle inequality** (ETS does not use this name): "The length of each side must be less than the sum of the lengths of the other two sides." Example: 4, 7, and 12 cannot be side lengths because $12 > 4 + 7$ (p. 96).
- Equilateral triangle: the three interior angles are equal, each $60^\circ$ (p. 97).
- Isosceles triangle: "If a triangle has two congruent sides, then the angles opposite the two congruent sides are congruent. The converse is also true." Example: $50 + 50 + x = 180$, so $\angle B = 80^\circ$ (p. 97).
- Pythagorean theorem for $\triangle DEF$ with right angle $D$: $(EF)^2 = (DE)^2 + (DF)^2$ (p. 98).
  - Example: $8^2 = 5^2 + x^2 \Rightarrow 64 = 25 + x^2 \Rightarrow 39 = x^2 \Rightarrow x = \sqrt{39} \approx 6.2$ ("$x$ must be positive") (p. 98).
- Isosceles right (45°-45°-90°) triangle: $y^2 = x^2 + x^2 \Rightarrow y^2 = 2x^2 \Rightarrow y = \sqrt{2}\,x$, so the sides are in the ratio $x : x : \sqrt{2}\,x$, which is the same as $1 : 1 : \sqrt{2}$ (ETS writes "1 to 1 to $\sqrt{2}$") (p. 99).
- 30°-60°-90° triangle: the side opposite $30^\circ$ ($x$) is one-half the hypotenuse ($2x$). Then $x^2 + y^2 = (2x)^2 \Rightarrow x^2 + y^2 = 4x^2 \Rightarrow y^2 = 3x^2 \Rightarrow y = \sqrt{3}\,x$, so the sides are in the ratio $x : \sqrt{3}\,x : 2x$, or $1 : \sqrt{3} : 2$ (pp. 99–100).
- Area of a triangle: $A = \dfrac{bh}{2}$, where $b$ is the length of a base and $h$ is the length of the corresponding height (p. 100).
- Congruence correspondence: "triangles $PQR$ and $STU$ are congruent" means $\angle P$ corresponds to $\angle S$, $\angle Q$ to $\angle T$, and $\angle R$ to $\angle U$; also $PQ$, $QR$, $PR$ correspond to $ST$, $TU$, $SU$ respectively (p. 101).
- Congruence tests: SSS, SAS, ASA, and AAS (AAS follows from ASA because the third angles must also be congruent, since the angles sum to $180^\circ$) (p. 101).
- All 30°-60°-90° right triangles are similar (p. 102).
- Similar triangles $ABC$ and $DEF$: $\dfrac{AB}{DE} = \dfrac{BC}{EF} = \dfrac{AC}{DF}$. By cross multiplication you can get other proportions, such as $\dfrac{AB}{BC} = \dfrac{DE}{EF}$ (p. 102).

### Gotchas
- **The letter order matters**: in "triangles $PQR$ and $STU$ are congruent" (or "similar"), the order of the letters tells you which parts correspond (pp. 101–102).
- Any side can be the base. The height may fall **outside** the triangle, onto the extension of the base (Figure 14, third triangle). In a right triangle, a leg can be the height (pp. 100–101).
- "Isosceles" means **at least** two congruent sides, so an equilateral triangle is also isosceles (p. 97).
- If a length must be positive, take the positive square root ($x = \sqrt{39}$, p. 98).
- Similarity can be checked with angles **or** with side ratios; the two are equivalent (p. 102).

### Facts / formulas
- The interior angles of a quadrilateral sum to $360^\circ$ (p. 102).
- Rectangle: opposite sides are parallel and congruent, and the two diagonals are congruent. In $ABCD$: $AD$ and $BC$ are parallel and congruent, $AB$ and $DC$ are parallel and congruent, and diagonal $AC$ is congruent to diagonal $BD$ (pp. 102–103).
- Parallelogram: opposite sides are congruent and opposite angles are congruent. In $PQRS$: $PQ$ and $SR$ are parallel and congruent; $QR$ and $PS$ are parallel and congruent; $\angle Q \cong \angle S$ (ETS writes "Opposite angles $Q$ and $S$ are congruent"); and $\angle P$ and $\angle R$ are congruent (p. 103).
- "Note that all rectangles are parallelograms." (p. 103) A square is a rectangle (p. 103).
- Area of any parallelogram (including rectangles and squares): $A = bh$ (p. 104).
- Area of a trapezoid: $A = \dfrac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2)(h)$, where $b_1$ and $b_2$ are the lengths of the bases and $h$ is the corresponding height. Example: $\dfrac{1}{2}(10 + 18)(7.5) = 105$ (p. 105).

### Gotchas
- The trapezoid definition says "**at least one** pair" of parallel sides, so under ETS's definition a parallelogram is also a trapezoid (p. 104). (The conclusion is our reading; ETS states only the definition.)
- In a parallelogram, the height is perpendicular to the base. It is generally **not** the slanted side (Figure 19) (p. 105).

### Facts / formulas
- $d = 2r$ (the diameter is twice the radius) (p. 106).
- $\dfrac{C}{d} = \pi$; $\pi \approx 3.14$, or $\pi \approx \dfrac{22}{7}$ (p. 106).
- $\dfrac{C}{d} = \dfrac{C}{2r} = \pi$, so $C = 2\pi r$ (pp. 106–107). Example: $r = 5.2$ gives $(2)(\pi)(5.2) = (10.4)(\pi) \approx (10.4)(3.14)$, which is about $32.7$ (p. 107).
- Measure of an arc = measure of its central angle. Whole circle = $360^\circ$. In Figure 23, arc $ABC = 50^\circ$ and arc $ADC = 310^\circ$ (pp. 107–108).
- Arc length: $\dfrac{\text{length of arc}}{\text{circumference}} = \dfrac{\text{degree measure of arc}}{360}$. Example: $\dfrac{\text{length of arc } ABC}{10\pi} = \dfrac{50}{360}$, so the length is $\left(\dfrac{50}{360}\right)(10\pi) = \dfrac{25\pi}{18} \approx \dfrac{(25)(3.14)}{18} \approx 4.4$ (p. 108).
- Area of a circle: $\pi r^2$. Example: $\pi(5^2) = 25\pi$ (p. 108).
- Sector area: $\dfrac{\text{area of sector}}{\text{area of circle}} = \dfrac{\text{degree measure of its arc}}{360}$. Example: $\dfrac{S}{25\pi} = \dfrac{50}{360}$, so $S = \left(\dfrac{50}{360}\right)(25\pi) = \dfrac{125\pi}{36} \approx \dfrac{(125)(3.14)}{36} \approx 10.9$ (p. 109).
- Compact forms (note, not ETS; ETS gives only the ratio rules): arc length $= \dfrac{\theta}{360}\cdot 2\pi r$ and sector area $= \dfrac{\theta}{360}\cdot \pi r^2$, where $\theta$ is the central angle in degrees.
- Tangent ⟂ radius: "If a line is tangent to a circle, then a radius drawn to the point of tangency is perpendicular to the tangent line." Converse: "if a radius and a line intersect at a point on the circle and the line is perpendicular to the radius, then the line is a tangent to the circle at the point of intersection." (p. 109)
- The center of a circle about an inscribed triangle may be inside the triangle, outside it, or on one of its sides. "If the center of the circle is on one of the sides of the inscribed triangle, that side is a diameter of the circle." (p. 110)
- Diameter and right triangle: "If one side of an inscribed triangle is a diameter of the circle, then the triangle is a right triangle. Conversely, if an inscribed triangle is a right triangle, then one of its sides is a diameter of the circle." In $\triangle XYZ$, $XZ$ is a diameter and $\angle Y$ is a right angle (p. 110).

### Gotchas
- Two points always determine **two** arcs. Name an arc by three points ($ABC$ versus $ADC$) to avoid ambiguity (p. 107).
- "Radius" and "diameter" mean both a **length** and a **segment** (p. 106).
- A diameter is also a chord (p. 106).
- Don't confuse the two meanings of "inscribed/circumscribed": polygon *in* circle (vertices on the circle) versus polygon *about* circle (sides tangent) (pp. 110–111).
- Keep answers in terms of $\pi$ (e.g. $25\pi$, $58.5\pi$) unless an approximation is asked for. The ETS answers are given in terms of $\pi$ (pp. 108, 114, 124).

### Figures
- **Geometry Figure 29 (p. 112):** a rectangular solid drawn in oblique view. The front face is a rectangle with a solid outline, and the hidden edges are **dashed**. Labels: $w$ under the front bottom edge, $\ell$ along the bottom-right receding edge, and $h$ along the right back vertical edge.

At both $P$ and $Q$ a horizontal radius is drawn to the rim (solid at the top, dashed at the bottom), with a small right-angle square between the axis and the radius.

### Facts / formulas
- A rectangular solid has 6 faces, 12 edges, and 8 vertices (p. 112).
- Rectangular solid volume: $V = \ell w h$ (p. 113).
- Rectangular solid surface area: $A = 2(\ell w + \ell h + wh)$ (p. 113).
  - Example: $\ell = 8.5$, $w = 5$, $h = 10$: $V = (8.5)(5)(10) = 425$; $A = 2\big((8.5)(5) + (8.5)(10) + (5)(10)\big) = 355$ (p. 113).
- Cube: $\ell = w = h$ (p. 112).
- Right circular cylinder volume ("the product of the height and the area of the base"): $V = \pi r^2 h$ (p. 114).
- Right circular cylinder surface area ("the sum of the areas of the two bases and the area of its lateral surface"): $A = 2(\pi r^2) + 2\pi r h$ (p. 114).
  - Example: $h = 6.5$, $r = 3$: $V = \pi(3^2)(6.5) = 58.5\pi$; $A = 2\pi(3^2) + 2\pi(3)(6.5) = 57\pi$ (p. 114).
- The height of a right circular cylinder equals the length of its axis $PQ$ (p. 113).
- Exercise 14(b) asks for the length of a space diagonal $AB$ of a rectangular solid. ETS gives no formula for it; the answer $3\sqrt{17}$ comes from applying the Pythagorean theorem twice (note, not ETS: $\sqrt{\ell^2 + w^2 + h^2} = \sqrt{100 + 49 + 4} = \sqrt{153} = 3\sqrt{17}$).

### Gotchas
- **No sphere, cone, or pyramid formulas** appear in the Math Review (p. 112). (note, not ETS: if a GRE question needs one, it is expected to give it; the site should not present those formulas as ETS content.)
- The surface area counts all six faces, in three equal pairs (p. 113).

## Relevant Math Conventions

Source: `conventions.pdf` (GRE Math Conventions, © 2024 ETS). Here the printed page = PDF page.

### Symbols (p. 4, p. 7)
- $\pi$ "represents the ratio of the circumference of a circle to its diameter and is approximately 3.14." (Numbers and Quantities 4, p. 4)
- Standard symbols table (Mathematical Expressions 5, p. 7):
  - $k \parallel m$: "lines $k$ and $m$ are parallel"
  - $k \perp m$: "lines $k$ and $m$ are perpendicular"
  - $\angle B$: "angle $B$"
  - Also relevant: $\sqrt{x}$ is "the nonnegative square root of $x$, where $x \ge 0$" (p. 6), and $x \approx y$ means "$x$ is approximately equal to $y$" (p. 6).
- Juxtaposition of letters sometimes does *not* mean multiplication; the context decides (Expressions 8, p. 7). (note, not ETS: this is why "$AB$" names a segment, not $A \times B$.)

### Geometry (p. 8)
1. "In questions involving geometry, the conventions of plane (or Euclidean) geometry are followed, including the assumption that the sum of the measures of the interior angles of a triangle is 180 degrees."
2. "Lines are assumed to be 'straight' lines that extend in both directions without end."
3. "Angle measures are in degrees and are assumed to be positive and less than or equal to 360 degrees."
4. "When a square, circle, polygon, or other closed geometric figure is described in words but not shown, the figure is assumed to enclose a convex region. It is also assumed that such a closed geometric figure is not just a single point or a line segment." Mathematical Conventions Figure 1 shows three shapes that a "quadrilateral" can**not** be: one with a gap in its outline (labeled "Not closed"), and a self-crossing bow-tie shape and a concave arrowhead/dart shape (labeled "Not convex").
5. "The phrase **area of a rectangle** means the area of the region enclosed by the rectangle. The same terminology applies to circles, triangles, and other closed figures."
6. "The **distance between a point and a line** is the length of the perpendicular line segment from the point to the line, which is the shortest distance between the point and the line. Similarly, the **distance between two parallel lines** is the distance between a point on one line and the other line."
7. "In a geometric context, the phrase **similar triangles** (or other figures) means that the figures have the same shape. See the Geometry part of the Math Review for further explanation of the terms **similar** and **congruent**."

### Geometric Figures (pp. 8–10)
1. "Geometric figures consist of points, lines, line segments, curves (such as circles), angles, and regions; also included are labels, markings, or shadings that identify these objects or their sizes. A point is indicated by a dot, a label, or the intersection of two or more lines or curves. Points, lines, angles, etc., that are shown as distinct are indeed distinct. All figures are assumed to lie in a plane unless otherwise indicated." (p. 8)
2. "If points $A$, $B$, and $C$ do not lie on the same line, then line segments $AB$ and $BC$ form two angles with vertex $B$: one angle with measure less than 180º and the other with measure greater than 180º … Unless otherwise indicated, angle $ABC$, also called angle $B$, refers to the *smaller* of the two angles." (pp. 8–9)
   - **Mathematical Conventions Figure 2 (p. 9):** horizontal segment from $A$ (left) to $B$, and segment from $B$ up and to the right to $C$. A small arc inside the angle is labeled "Less than 180°", and a larger arc going around the outside of $B$ is labeled "Greater than 180°".
3. "The notation $AB$ may mean the line segment with endpoints $A$ and $B$, or it may mean the length of the line segment. It may also mean the line containing points $A$ and $B$. The meaning can be determined from the context." (p. 9)
4. "Geometric figures *are not necessarily* drawn to scale. That is, you should *not* assume that quantities such as lengths and angle measures are as they appear in a figure. However, you should assume that lines shown as straight are actually straight, and when curves are shown, you should assume they are not straight. Also, assume that points on a line or a curve are in the order shown, points shown to be on opposite sides of a line or curve are so oriented, and more generally, assume all geometric objects are in the relative positions shown. For questions with geometric figures, you should base your answers on geometric reasoning, not on estimating or comparing quantities by sight or by measurement." (p. 9)
   - **Mathematical Conventions Figure 3 (p. 9):** triangle $ABC$ with $A$ at the bottom left, $B$ at the top, and $C$ at the bottom right. Point $D$ is on $AC$ and a segment $BD$ is drawn; point $E$ is a dot on $BD$. A small right-angle square is at $B$ (for angle $ABC$). Angle $ABD$ is labeled $x^\circ$, and the angle at $C$ is labeled $35^\circ$. $DC$ is labeled $6$.
     - **May be assumed** (pp. 9–10): (1) $A$, $D$, $C$ are distinct, $D$ lies between $A$ and $C$, and the line containing them is straight; (2) $AD < AC$; (3) $ABC$, $ABD$, and $DBC$ are triangles; (4) $E$ lies on segment $BD$; (5) angle $ABC$ is a right angle, "as indicated by the small square symbol at point $B$"; (6) "The length of line segment $DC$ is 6, and the measure of angle $C$ is 35 degrees"; (7) the measure of angle $ABD$ is $x$ degrees, and $x < 90$.
     - **May NOT be assumed** (p. 10): (1) $AD > DC$; (2) the measures of angles $BAD$ and $BDA$ are equal; (3) the measure of angle $DBC$ is less than $x$ degrees; (4) the area of $\triangle ABD$ is greater than the area of $\triangle DBC$.
   - **Mathematical Conventions Figure 4 (p. 10):** a closed oval-shaped curve. Line $k$ passes through $S$ (upper left on the curve) and $T$ (lower right on the curve), and the segment $ST$ is labeled $9$. Line $m$ is horizontal through $R$ (lower left on the curve) and $T$. A segment joins $R$ and $S$ (forming triangle $RST$). Point $U$ is on the curve below line $m$.
     - **May be assumed** (p. 10): (1) $R$, $S$, $T$, $U$ lie on a closed curve; (2) line $k$ intersects the closed curve at $S$ and $T$; (3) $S$ and $U$ are on opposite sides of line $m$; (4) the length of side $ST$ is 9; (5) the area of the region enclosed by the curve is greater than the area of triangle $RST$.
     - **May NOT be assumed**: "angle $SRT$ is a right angle" is "consistent with the way the figure is drawn, but you should *not* assume" it (p. 10). The angle looks right but has no square mark.

### Coordinate Systems (p. 11), the geometry-related parts
1. "Coordinate systems, such as $xy$-planes and number lines, *are* drawn to scale. Therefore, you can read, estimate, or compare quantities in such figures by sight or by measurement, including geometric figures that appear in coordinate systems." (This is the exception to "not drawn to scale".)
3. "As in geometry, distances in a coordinate system are nonnegative."
