## 1.1 Integers (Math Review pp. 3–7)

### Definitions
- **Integers** (p. 3): "The integers are the numbers 1, 2, 3, …, together with their negatives, −1, −2, −3, …, and 0." So the set of integers is $\{\ldots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots\}$.
- **Positive / negative integers** (p. 3): "The positive integers are greater than 0, the negative integers are less than 0, and 0 is neither positive nor negative."
- **Factor / divisor** (p. 3): "When integers are multiplied, each of the multiplied integers is called a factor or divisor of the resulting product." Example: $(2)(3)(10) = 60$, so 2, 3, and 10 are factors of 60.
- **Multiple / divisible** (p. 3): "We say that 60 is a multiple of each of its factors and that 60 is divisible by each of its divisors."
- **Least common multiple** (p. 4): "The least common multiple of two nonzero integers $c$ and $d$ is the least positive integer that is a multiple of both $c$ and $d$." Example: LCM of 30 and 75 is 150.
- **Greatest common divisor (greatest common factor)** (p. 4): "The greatest common divisor (or greatest common factor) of two nonzero integers $c$ and $d$ is the greatest positive integer that is a divisor of both $c$ and $d$." Example: GCD of 30 and 75 is 15.
- **Quotient and remainder** (pp. 4–5): if $d$ is *not* a divisor of $c$, the result of dividing $c$ by $d$ "can be viewed as a fraction or as a decimal … or the result can be viewed as a **quotient** with a **remainder**, where both are integers." ETS's procedure: "when an integer $c$ is divided by a positive integer $d$, you first find the greatest multiple of $d$ that is less than or equal to $c$. That multiple of $d$ can be expressed as the product $qd$, where $q$ is the quotient. Then the remainder is equal to $c$ minus that multiple of $d$, or $r = c - qd$, where $r$ is the remainder. The remainder is always greater than or equal to 0 and less than $d$." (p. 5)
- **Even integer / odd integer** (p. 6): "If an integer is divisible by 2, it is called an even integer; otherwise, it is an odd integer." Even integers are $\{\ldots, -6, -4, -2, 0, 2, 4, 6, \ldots\}$ and odd integers are $\{\ldots, -5, -3, -1, 1, 3, 5, \ldots\}$.
- **Prime number** (p. 6): "A prime number is an integer greater than 1 that has only two positive divisors: 1 and itself." ETS lists the first ten primes: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 23, and 29.
- **Prime divisors / prime factorization** (p. 6): "Every integer greater than 1 either is a prime number or can be uniquely expressed as a product of factors that are prime numbers, or **prime divisors**. Such an expression is called a **prime factorization**."
- **Composite number** (p. 7): "An integer greater than 1 that is not a prime number is called a composite number." ETS lists the first ten: 4, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 15, 16, and 18.

### Facts / rules / formulas
- Closure (p. 3): when integers are added, subtracted, or multiplied, the result is always an integer. Division of integers is handled separately (quotient and remainder, fractions, decimals).
- Sign facts for multiplication (p. 3):
  - Fact 1: The product of two positive integers is a positive integer.
  - Fact 2: The product of two negative integers is a positive integer.
  - Fact 3: The product of a positive integer and a negative integer is a negative integer.
- A factor's negative is also a factor (p. 3): the positive factors of 60 are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, 60, and "the negatives of these integers are also factors of 60", e.g. $(-2)(-30) = 60$.
- Examples 1.1.1–1.1.5 (pp. 3–4):
  - The positive factors of 100 are 1, 2, 4, 5, 10, 20, 25, 50, 100.
  - "25 is a multiple of only six integers: 1, 5, 25, and their negatives."
  - "The list of positive multiples of 25 has no end … likewise, every nonzero integer has infinitely many multiples."
  - "1 is a factor of every integer; 1 is not a multiple of any integer except 1 and −1."
  - "0 is a multiple of every integer; 0 is not a factor of any integer except 0."
- Divisor quotient (p. 4): "When an integer $c$ is divided by an integer $d$, where $d$ is a divisor of $c$, the result is always a divisor of $c$." Example: $60 \div 6 = 10$.
- Division with remainder (p. 5): $c = qd + r$, that is $r = c - qd$, with $0 \le r < d$ (for a positive divisor $d$).
- Remainder zero (p. 5, Ex. 1.1.7): "the remainder is 0 if and only if $c$ is divisible by $d$."
- Worked cases (p. 5):
  - $100 \div 45$ is 2 remainder 10.
  - $24 \div 4$ is 6 remainder 0.
  - $6 \div 24$ is 0 remainder 6, so a smaller dividend gives quotient 0 and the dividend is the remainder.
  - $-32 \div 3$ is $-11$ remainder 1, since $(-11)(3) = -33 \le -32$.
  - $100 = (33)(3) + 1$.
  - $100 = (4)(25) + 0$.
  - $80 = (0)(100) + 80$.
  - $-13 = (-3)(5) + 2$.
  - $-73 = (-8)(10) + 7$.
- Odd divided by 2 (p. 6): "when an odd integer is divided by 2, the remainder is always 1."
- Even/odd sum and product facts (p. 6):
  - Fact 1: even + even = even.
  - Fact 2: odd + odd = even.
  - Fact 3: even + odd = odd.
  - Fact 4: even × even = even.
  - Fact 5: odd × odd = odd.
  - Fact 6: even × odd = even.
- Primes (p. 6): 14 is not prime (positive divisors 1, 2, 7, 14). "The integer 1 is not a prime number, and the integer 2 is the only prime number that is even."
- Prime factorization examples (pp. 6–7):
  - $12 = (2)(2)(3) = (2^2)(3)$
  - $14 = (2)(7)$
  - $81 = (3)(3)(3)(3) = 3^4$
  - $338 = (2)(13)(13) = (2)(13^2)$
  - $800 = (2)(2)(2)(2)(2)(5)(5) = (2^5)(5^2)$
  - $1{,}155 = (3)(5)(7)(11)$

### Notation / conventions
- ETS writes products with parentheses, e.g. $(2)(3)(10)$, and writes prime factorizations as products of prime powers, e.g. $(2^5)(5^2)$.
- "$c$ divided by $d$ is $q$ remainder $r$", e.g. "19 divided by 7 is the quotient 2 with remainder 5, or simply 2 remainder 5" (p. 5).
- Remainders with a negative dividend (p. 5): the quotient is found from "the greatest multiple of $d$ that is less than or equal to $c$", so the remainder is still nonnegative. For example, $-32 \div 3$ gives $q = -11$, $r = 1$ (not $-10$ remainder $-2$). Likewise $-13 \div 5$ gives $-3$ remainder 2, and $-73 \div 10$ gives $-8$ remainder 7.
- The Math Review states the remainder procedure only for a **positive** divisor $d$. The Math Conventions (p. 5) extend it to any nonzero $d$ with $0 \le r < |d|$; see the conventions section at the end of this file.
- LCM and GCD are defined for two **nonzero** integers and are always **positive** (p. 4).

### Exercise styles
Exercises 1, 3–7, 14, 15 (pp. 28–31):
- evaluating integer expressions with signs, order of operations and powers;
- testing divisibility (by 8);
- finding prime factorizations, all positive divisors, and prime divisors;
- picking out the primes from a list;
- deciding whether expressions in an even $c$ and an odd $d$ are even or odd (including $c^d$, $(c+d)^2$ and $c^2-d^2$);
- finding the least positive integer that has given remainders on division by 3 and by 5.

### Gotchas
- 0 is neither positive nor negative (p. 3).
- 0 is even, since the set of even integers includes 0 (p. 6). Negative integers can be even or odd.
- 1 is not prime, and 2 is the only even prime (p. 6). Primes and composites are both defined only for integers **greater than 1**, so 1, 0 and negative integers are neither prime nor composite. (note, not ETS: this follows directly from the two definitions.)
- Factors include negatives: 25 has six integer factors, not three (p. 4).
- 0 is a multiple of every integer, but 0 is a factor only of 0. 1 is a factor of every integer, but 1 is a multiple only of $\pm 1$ (p. 4).
- The remainder is always $\ge 0$ and $< d$, even when the dividend is negative. Example: $-32 = (-11)(3) + 1$ (p. 5).
- If the dividend is smaller than a positive divisor (and nonnegative), the quotient is 0 and the remainder is the dividend: $6 \div 24$ is 0 remainder 6, and $80 \div 100$ is 0 remainder 80 (p. 5).
- A prime factorization is unique (p. 6).

## 1.2 Fractions (Math Review pp. 7–11)

### Definitions
- **Fraction / numerator / denominator** (p. 7): "A fraction is a number of the form $\frac{c}{d}$, where $c$ and $d$ are integers and $d \neq 0$. The integer $c$ is called the numerator of the fraction, and $d$ is called the denominator." Example: in $\frac{-7}{5}$, $-7$ is the numerator and 5 is the denominator.
- **Rational numbers** (p. 7): fractions "are also called rational numbers. Note that every integer $n$ is a rational number, because $n$ is equal to the fraction $\frac{n}{1}$."
- **Common denominator** (p. 8): "a common multiple of the two denominators."
- **Invert / reciprocal** (p. 9): to divide fractions, "first invert the second fraction (that is, find its reciprocal)."
- **Mixed number** (p. 9): "An expression such as $4\frac{3}{8}$ is called a mixed number. It consists of an integer part and a fraction part, where the fraction part has a value between 0 and 1; the mixed number $4\frac{3}{8}$ means $4 + \frac{3}{8}$."
- **Fractional expressions** (p. 10): "Numbers of the form $\frac{c}{d}$, where either $c$ or $d$ is not an integer and $d \neq 0$, are called fractional expressions. Fractional expressions can be manipulated just like fractions."

### Facts / rules / formulas
- Equivalent fractions (p. 7): if both $c$ and $d$ ($d \neq 0$) are multiplied by the same nonzero integer, the result is equivalent to $\frac{c}{d}$:
  - $\frac{-7}{5} = \frac{(-7)(4)}{(5)(4)} = \frac{-28}{20}$
  - $\frac{-7}{5} = \frac{(-7)(-1)}{(5)(-1)} = \frac{7}{-5}$
- Sign placement (p. 8): "For all integers $c$ and $d$, the fractions $\frac{-c}{d}$, $\frac{c}{-d}$, and $-\frac{c}{d}$ are equivalent." Example: $\frac{-7}{5} = \frac{7}{-5} = -\frac{7}{5}$. (note, not ETS: as a fraction, $d$ must still be nonzero.)
- Reducing (p. 8): if the numerator and denominator have a common factor, they can be factored and the fraction reduced to an equivalent fraction: $\frac{40}{72} = \frac{(8)(5)}{(8)(9)} = \frac{5}{9}$.
- Adding with the same denominator (p. 8): add the numerators and keep the denominator: $-\frac{8}{11} + \frac{5}{11} = \frac{-8+5}{11} = \frac{-3}{11} = -\frac{3}{11}$.
- Adding with different denominators (p. 8): find a common denominator, convert both fractions to it, then add the numerators and keep the common denominator:
  $$\frac{1}{3} + \frac{-2}{5} = \frac{5}{15} + \frac{-6}{15} = \frac{5 + (-6)}{15} = -\frac{1}{15}$$
  "The same method applies to subtraction of fractions." (p. 9)
- Multiplying (p. 9): multiply the numerators and multiply the denominators.
  - $\left(\frac{10}{7}\right)\left(\frac{-1}{3}\right) = \frac{(10)(-1)}{(7)(3)} = \frac{-10}{21} = -\frac{10}{21}$
  - $\left(\frac{8}{3}\right)\left(\frac{7}{3}\right) = \frac{56}{9}$
- Dividing (p. 9): invert the second fraction, then multiply.
  - $\frac{17}{8} \div \frac{3}{5} = \left(\frac{17}{8}\right)\left(\frac{5}{3}\right) = \frac{85}{24}$
  - $\dfrac{\frac{3}{10}}{\frac{7}{13}} = \left(\frac{3}{10}\right)\left(\frac{13}{7}\right) = \frac{39}{70}$
- Mixed number to fraction (p. 10): convert the integer part to an equivalent fraction with the same denominator as the fraction part, then add:
  $$4\tfrac{3}{8} = \frac{32}{8} + \frac{3}{8} = \frac{35}{8}$$
- Fractional expressions (p. 10–11):
  - $\frac{\pi}{2} + \frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{3\pi}{6} + \frac{2\pi}{6} = \frac{5\pi}{6}$
  - $\dfrac{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}}{\frac{3}{\sqrt{5}}} = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\sqrt{5}}{3}\right) = \frac{\sqrt{5}}{3\sqrt{2}}$ (ETS leaves this answer unrationalized).

### Notation / conventions
- ETS uses the horizontal fraction bar for division and parenthesized factors for products.
- A mixed number $4\frac{3}{8}$ means $4 + \frac{3}{8}$, not $4 \times \frac{3}{8}$ (p. 9).
- Complex fractions (a fraction over a fraction) are simplified by multiplying by the reciprocal of the denominator (pp. 9, 11).

### Exercise styles
Exercise 2 (p. 28) and Exercise 8(h), (j) (pp. 29–30):
- evaluating sums, differences, products, quotients and squares of fractions, including a negative denominator such as $\frac{3}{-8}$;
- judging the truth of statements about fraction equivalence and comparison.

### Gotchas
- The denominator can never be 0 ($d \neq 0$, p. 7).
- A negative sign can sit in the numerator, the denominator, or in front; all three forms are equal (p. 8).
- Every integer is a rational number, because $n = \frac{n}{1}$ (p. 7).
- The fraction part of a mixed number lies between 0 and 1 (p. 9).
- "Fraction" in ETS's sense requires an integer numerator and denominator. Something like $\frac{\pi}{2}$ is a "fractional expression", though it obeys the same rules (p. 10).

## 1.3 Exponents and Roots (Math Review pp. 11–14)

### Definitions
- **Exponent / base** (p. 11): "Exponents are used to denote the repeated multiplication of a number by itself; for example, $3^4 = (3)(3)(3)(3) = 81$ and $5^3 = (5)(5)(5) = 125$. In the expression $3^4$, 3 is called the base, 4 is called the exponent, and we read the expression as '3 to the fourth power.'"
- **Squaring** (p. 12): "When the exponent is 2, we call the process squaring." Examples: $6^2 = 36$ and $7^2 = 49$.
- **Zero exponent** (p. 12): "For all nonzero numbers $a$, $a^0 = 1$. The expression $0^0$ is undefined."
- **Negative exponents** (p. 12): "For all nonzero numbers $a$, $a^{-1} = \frac{1}{a}$, $a^{-2} = \frac{1}{a^2}$, $a^{-3} = \frac{1}{a^3}$, and so on." Note that $(a)(a^{-1}) = (a)\left(\frac{1}{a}\right) = 1$.
- **Square root** (p. 12): "A square root of a nonnegative number $n$ is a number $r$ such that $r^2 = n$." Example: 4 and $-4$ are both square roots of 16.
- **The symbol $\sqrt{\ }$** (p. 12): "The expression consisting of the square root symbol $\sqrt{\ }$ placed over a nonnegative number denotes the *nonnegative* square root (or the positive square root if the number is greater than 0) of that nonnegative number." So $\sqrt{100} = 10$, $-\sqrt{100} = -10$, and $\sqrt{0} = 0$.
- **Cube root / fourth root (higher order roots)** (p. 13): "A square root is a root of order 2. Higher order roots of a positive number $n$ are defined similarly. For orders 3 and 4, the cube root of $n$, written as $\sqrt[3]{n}$, and fourth root of $n$, written as $\sqrt[4]{n}$, represent numbers such that when they are raised to the powers 3 and 4, respectively, the result is $n$."

### Facts / rules / formulas
- Negative bases (p. 12): $(-3)^2 = (-3)(-3) = 9$ and $(-3)^5 = -243$. "A negative number raised to an even power is always positive, and a negative number raised to an odd power is always negative."
- Parentheses matter (p. 12): $(-3)^2 = 9$, but $-3^2 = -((3)(3)) = -9$.
- Square roots, counted (p. 12):
  - "All positive numbers have two square roots, one positive and one negative."
  - "The only square root of 0 is 0."
  - "Square roots of negative numbers are not defined in the real number system."
- Four rules for square roots, "where $a > 0$ and $b > 0$" (p. 13):
  - Rule 1: $\left(\sqrt{a}\right)^2 = a$. Examples: $\left(\sqrt{3}\right)^2 = 3$ and $\left(\sqrt{\pi}\right)^2 = \pi$.
  - Rule 2: $\sqrt{a^2} = a$. Examples: $\sqrt{4} = \sqrt{2^2} = 2$ and $\sqrt{\pi^2} = \pi$.
  - Rule 3: $\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$. Examples: $\sqrt{3}\sqrt{10} = \sqrt{30}$ and $\sqrt{24} = \sqrt{4}\sqrt{6} = 2\sqrt{6}$.
  - Rule 4: $\dfrac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{a}{b}}$. Examples: $\frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{15}} = \sqrt{\frac{5}{15}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{3}}$ and $\frac{\sqrt{18}}{\sqrt{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{18}{2}} = \sqrt{9} = 3$.
- Higher roots (p. 13): "These roots obey rules similar to those above but with the exponent 2 replaced by 3 or 4 in the first two rules." That is, $\left(\sqrt[3]{a}\right)^3 = a$, $\sqrt[3]{a^3} = a$, $\left(\sqrt[4]{a}\right)^4 = a$, and $\sqrt[4]{a^4} = a$ (for $a > 0$).
- Odd and even order roots, "in the real number system" (p. 14):
  - "For odd order roots, there is *exactly one* root for *every* number $n$, even when $n$ is negative."
  - "For even order roots, there are *exactly two* roots for every *positive* number $n$ and *no* roots for any *negative* number $n$."
  - Examples: $\sqrt[3]{8} = 2$; 8 has two fourth roots, $\sqrt[4]{8}$ and $-\sqrt[4]{8}$; $\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2$; $-8$ has no fourth root.
- Exponent laws such as $a^m a^n = a^{m+n}$ are **not** stated in 1.3. They are in 2.2 Rules of Exponents (p. 40). The Arithmetic answer key does use the product rule, in Exercise 8(k) (p. 34): $(59^3)(59^2) = 59^{3+2} = 59^5$.

### Notation / conventions
- $-3^2$ means $-(3^2)$ (p. 12). See also the order-of-operations convention at the end of this file.
- $\sqrt{x}$ always means the nonnegative root, and $-\sqrt{x}$ is the negative root (p. 12).
- $\sqrt[3]{n}$ and $\sqrt[4]{n}$ denote the cube root and fourth root (p. 13).

### Exercise styles
Exercise 1(d), (f), (g) and Exercise 8(b), (f), (g), (k), (l) (pp. 28–30):
- evaluating powers of negative numbers;
- true/false items on $\sqrt{16}$, $(-1)^{87}$, $\sqrt{(-3)^2}$, products of powers, and $-\sqrt{25} < -4$.

### Gotchas
- $(-3)^2 = 9$ but $-3^2 = -9$ (p. 12).
- $0^0$ is undefined, and zero or negative exponents are defined only for **nonzero** bases (p. 12).
- $\sqrt{16} = 4$ only. Although $-4$ is a square root of 16, the symbol denotes the nonnegative root (p. 12).
- Rule 2 ($\sqrt{a^2} = a$) is stated for $a > 0$ (p. 13). The answer key shows $\sqrt{(-3)^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3$, not $-3$ (p. 33, Ex. 8(g)).
- There are no real square roots, or other even-order roots, of negative numbers. Odd-order roots of negatives exist and are negative (p. 14).
- An even power of a negative number is positive, and an odd power is negative (p. 12). For example, $(-1)^{87} = -1$ (Ex. 8(f), answer p. 33).

## 1.4 Decimals (Math Review pp. 14–16)

### Definitions
- **Decimal number system / place value** (p. 14): "The decimal number system is based on representing numbers using powers of 10. The place value of each digit corresponds to a power of 10."
- Place names, from Arithmetic Figure 1 (p. 14), for 7,532.418:

  | Digit | Place |
  |---|---|
  | 7 | thousands |
  | 5 | hundreds |
  | 3 | tens |
  | 2 | ones (units) |
  | 4 | tenths |
  | 1 | hundredths |
  | 8 | thousandths |

- **Terminate / repeat** (p. 15): the decimal from long division of a fraction "will either terminate, as in $\frac{1}{4} = 0.25$ and $\frac{52}{25} = 2.08$, or repeat without end, as in $\frac{1}{9} = 0.111\ldots$, $\frac{1}{22} = 0.0454545\ldots$, and $\frac{25}{12} = 2.08333\ldots$."
- **Irrational numbers** (p. 16): decimals that do not terminate or repeat "are not rational numbers. Such numbers are called irrational numbers." Examples: $\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356237\ldots$, and $0.020220222022220\ldots$ ("groups of consecutive 2s separated by a 0, where the number of 2s in each successive group increases by one").

### Facts / rules / formulas
- Expanded form (pp. 14–15):
  $$7{,}532.418 = 7(1{,}000) + 5(100) + 3(10) + 2(1) + 4\left(\tfrac{1}{10}\right) + 1\left(\tfrac{1}{100}\right) + 8\left(\tfrac{1}{1{,}000}\right)$$
  $$= 7(10^3) + 5(10^2) + 3(10^1) + 2(10^0) + 4(10^{-1}) + 1(10^{-2}) + 8(10^{-3})$$
- Decimal to fraction (p. 15): with finitely many digits after the decimal point, "every decimal can be converted to an integer divided by a power of 10."
  - $2.3 = 2 + \frac{3}{10} = \frac{23}{10}$
  - $90.17 = 90 + \frac{17}{100} = \frac{9{,}017}{100}$
  - $0.612 = \frac{612}{1{,}000}$
- Fraction to decimal (p. 15): divide the numerator by the denominator using long division ("which is not in this review").
  - $\frac{3}{8} = 0.375$
  - $\frac{259}{40} = 6.475$
  - $-\frac{1}{3} = -0.\overline{3}$
  - $\frac{15}{14} = 1.0\overline{714285}$
- Rational ⇔ terminating or repeating (p. 16): "Every fraction with integers in the numerator and denominator is equivalent to a decimal that either terminates or repeats. That is, every rational number can be expressed as a terminating or repeating decimal. The converse is also true; that is, every terminating or repeating decimal represents a rational number."

### Notation / conventions
- A repeating block is shown with a bar over the repeating digits (p. 15), e.g. $-0.\overline{3}$ and $1.0\overline{714285}$. Note that in $\frac{15}{14}$ the 0 is not under the bar.
- Commas group digits in thousands (7,532.418; 9,017; 1,000), and a period is the decimal point.
- The Math Review gives **no rounding rule**. Rounding is covered only in the Math Conventions (see below).

### Exercise styles
Exercise 8(e) (p. 29) compares a decimal with a fraction ($0.3 < \frac{1}{3}$). Exercise 9(c) (p. 30) involves decimal percents. Section 1.4 has no dedicated exercise.

### Gotchas
- A decimal may terminate, repeat, or neither. Only the third kind is irrational (p. 16).
- A decimal with a pattern, such as $0.0202202220\ldots$, can still be irrational if it never repeats a fixed block (p. 16).
- $0.3 < \frac{1}{3} = 0.\overline{3}$ (Ex. 8(e) is True, p. 33).

## 1.5 Real Numbers (Math Review pp. 16–20)

### Definitions
- **Real numbers** (p. 16): "The set of real numbers consists of all rational numbers and all irrational numbers. The real numbers include all integers, fractions, and decimals."
- **Real number line** (p. 16): "The set of real numbers can be represented by a number line called the real number line." "Every real number corresponds to a point on the number line, and every point on the number line corresponds to a real number." Numbers left of 0 are negative, numbers right of 0 are positive, and "Only the number 0 is neither negative nor positive."
- **Less than / greater than** (pp. 16–17): "A real number $x$ is less than a real number $y$ if $x$ is to the left of $y$ on the number line, which is written as $x < y$. A real number $y$ is greater than a real number $x$ if $y$ is to the right of $x$ on the number line, which is written as $y > x$."
- **Less than or equal to / greater than or equal to** (p. 17): $x \le y$ if "$x$ is to the left of, *or corresponds to the same point as,* $y$ on the number line". $y \ge x$ is defined analogously ("to the right of, or corresponds to the same point as").
- **Between** (p. 17): "To say that a real number $x$ is between 2 and 3 on the number line means that $x > 2$ and $x < 3$, which can also be written as $2 < x < 3$."
- **Interval** (p. 17): "The set of all real numbers that are between 2 and 3 is called an interval, and $2 < x < 3$ is often used to represent that interval." The endpoints 2 and 3 are not included.
- **Absolute value** (p. 18): "The distance between a number $x$ and 0 on the number line is called the absolute value of $x$, written as $|x|$."
- **Triangle inequality** (p. 19): Property 10, $|r + s| \le |r| + |s|$.

### Facts / rules / formulas
- Relationships read from Arithmetic Figure 2 (p. 17): $-\sqrt{5} < -2$, $\frac{1}{2} > 0$, and $1 < \sqrt{2} < 2$.
- Intervals with two endpoints (p. 17):
  - $2 < x < 3$
  - $2 \le x < 3$
  - $2 < x \le 3$
  - $2 \le x \le 3$
- Intervals with one endpoint (pp. 17–18), each consisting of all real numbers to one side of the endpoint, with or without it:
  - $x < 4$
  - $x \le 4$
  - $x > 4$
  - $x \ge 4$
- "The entire real number line is also considered to be an interval." (p. 18)
- Absolute value (p. 18):
  - $|3| = 3$ and $|-3| = 3$.
  - If $x$ is positive, $|x| = x$. If $x$ is negative, $|x| = -x$. Also $|0| = 0$.
  - "It follows that the absolute value of any nonzero number is positive."
  - Examples: $\left|\sqrt{5}\right| = \sqrt{5}$, $|-23| = -(-23) = 23$, and $|-10.2| = 10.2$.
- Twelve properties of real numbers, where $r$, $s$, $t$ are real numbers (pp. 18–20):
  1. $r + s = s + r$ and $rs = sr$. Examples: $8 + 2 = 2 + 8 = 10$ and $(-3)(17) = (17)(-3) = -51$.
  2. $(r + s) + t = r + (s + t)$ and $(rs)t = r(st)$. Examples: $(7+3)+8 = 7+(3+8) = 18$ and $(7\sqrt{2})\sqrt{2} = 7(\sqrt{2}\sqrt{2}) = (7)(2) = 14$.
  3. $r(s + t) = rs + rt$. Example: $5(3+16) = (5)(3) + (5)(16) = 95$.
  4. $r + 0 = r$, $(r)(0) = 0$, and $(r)(1) = r$.
  5. If $rs = 0$, then either $r = 0$ or $s = 0$ or both. Example: if $-2s = 0$, then $s = 0$.
  6. Division by 0 is undefined. Examples: $5 \div 0$, $\frac{-7}{0}$ and $\frac{0}{0}$ are all undefined.
  7. If both $r$ and $s$ are positive, then both $r + s$ and $rs$ are positive.
  8. If both $r$ and $s$ are negative, then $r + s$ is negative and $rs$ is positive.
  9. If $r$ is positive and $s$ is negative, then $rs$ is negative.
  10. $|r + s| \le |r| + |s|$ (the **triangle inequality**). Example: $r = 5$, $s = -2$ gives $|5 + (-2)| = 3$ and $|5| + |-2| = 7$.
  11. $|r||s| = |rs|$. Example: $|5||-2| = |(5)(-2)| = |-10| = 10$.
  12. If $r > 1$, then $r^2 > r$. If $0 < s < 1$, then $s^2 < s$. Example: $5^2 = 25 > 5$, but $\left(\frac{1}{5}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{25} < \frac{1}{5}$.

### Notation / conventions
- The inequality symbols are $<$, $>$, $\le$ and $\ge$. A compound inequality $2 < x < 3$ means "$x > 2$ and $x < 3$" (p. 17).
- An interval is represented by its inequality. ETS does **not** use bracket or parenthesis interval notation such as $[2,3)$ in this section.
- "Between" excludes the endpoints unless $\le$ or $\ge$ is used (p. 17).

### Exercise styles
Exercise 8 (pp. 29–30): true/false statements about order on the number line, absolute values ($0 < \left|-\frac{1}{7}\right|$, $-|-23| = 23$), division by 0, and comparisons involving roots.

### Gotchas
- $7 \div 0 = 0$ is **false**, because division by 0 is undefined (Ex. 8(c), p. 33). $\frac{0}{0}$ is also undefined (p. 19).
- $-|-23| = -23$, not 23 (Ex. 8(i), p. 34).
- $|x| = -x$ when $x$ is negative, so $-x$ is then positive (p. 18).
- Squaring makes numbers between 0 and 1 smaller and numbers greater than 1 larger (Property 12, p. 20).
- In the triangle inequality, equality need not hold. The example shows strict inequality, $3 < 7$ (p. 19).
- On the number line, $-\sqrt{5} < -2$: a negative number with greater magnitude is the lesser number (p. 17).

## 1.6 Ratio (Math Review pp. 20–21)

### Definitions
- **Ratio** (p. 20): "The ratio of one quantity to another is a way to express their relative sizes, often in the form of a fraction, where the first quantity is the numerator and the second quantity is the denominator. Thus, if $s$ and $t$ are positive quantities, then the ratio of $s$ to $t$ can be written as the fraction $\frac{s}{t}$. The notation '$s$ to $t$' and the notation '$s : t$' are also used to express this ratio."
- **Ratio of three or more quantities** (p. 20): "If three or more positive quantities are being considered, say $r$, $s$, and $t$, then their relative sizes can also be expressed as a ratio with the notation '$r$ to $s$ to $t$.'"
- **Proportion** (p. 20): "A proportion is an equation relating two ratios; for example, $\frac{9}{12} = \frac{3}{4}$."
- **Cross multiplication** (p. 20): "To solve a problem involving ratios, you can often write a proportion and solve it by cross multiplication."

### Facts / rules / formulas
- Ratio notation (p. 20): 2 apples and 3 oranges gives a ratio of apples to oranges of $\frac{2}{3}$, or "2 to 3", or $2 : 3$.
- Reducing ratios (p. 20): "Like fractions, ratios can be reduced to lowest terms." 8 apples to 12 oranges is still 2 to 3, and 9 to 12 is equivalent to 3 to 4.
- Reducing a three-term ratio (p. 20): 5 to 30 to 20 reduces to 1 to 6 to 4, "by dividing each number by the greatest common divisor of 5, 30, and 20, which is 5."
- Cross multiplication (p. 21, Ex. 1.6.1):
  $$\frac{x}{49} = \frac{3}{21} \;\Rightarrow\; 21x = (3)(49) \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{(3)(49)}{21} = 7$$

### Notation / conventions
- The order of the ratio follows the order of the words: the first quantity named is the numerator (p. 20).
- ETS defines ratios for **positive** quantities.
- ETS uses three equivalent notations: $\frac{s}{t}$, "$s$ to $t$", and $s : t$. For three or more quantities only "$r$ to $s$ to $t$" is shown (p. 20).

### Exercise styles
Exercise 13 (p. 31): given a total count and a part-to-part ratio, find one of the parts.

### Gotchas
- A ratio is about relative size, not actual counts: 8 to 12 equals 2 to 3 (p. 20).
- The order matters: the ratio of $s$ to $t$ is $\frac{s}{t}$, not $\frac{t}{s}$ (p. 20).
- A multi-term ratio reduces by the GCD of **all** its terms (p. 20).

## 1.7 Percent (Math Review pp. 21–27)

### Definitions
- **Percent** (p. 21): "The term percent means *per hundred*, or *hundredths*. Percents are ratios that are often used to represent *parts of a whole*, where the whole is considered as having 100 parts. Percents can be converted to fraction or decimal equivalents."
- **Part / whole** (p. 21): "in the fraction equivalent, the *part* is the numerator of the fraction and the *whole* is the denominator."
- **Percent symbol** (p. 21): "Percents are often written using the percent symbol, %, instead of the word 'percent.'"
- **Base** (p. 24): "In general, the whole is called the base of the percent. When the numerator of a percent is greater than the base, the percent is greater than 100%."
- **Percent change** (p. 24): "When a quantity changes from an initial positive amount to another positive amount … you can compute the amount of change as a percent of the initial amount. This is called percent change."
- **Percent increase** (p. 25): "found by dividing the amount of increase by the base."
- **Percent decrease** (p. 25): computed as the amount of decrease divided by the base.

### Facts / rules / formulas
- Conversions (pp. 21–22):
  - 1 percent $= \frac{1}{100} = 0.01$
  - 32 percent $= \frac{32}{100} = 0.32$
  - 50 percent $= \frac{50}{100} = 0.50$
  - $100\% = \frac{100}{100} = 1$
  - $12\% = \frac{12}{100} = 0.12$
  - $8\% = \frac{8}{100} = 0.08$
  - $10\% = \frac{10}{100} = 0.1$
  - $0.3\% = \frac{0.3}{100} = 0.003$
- Percent symbol matters (p. 22): "Be careful not to confuse 0.01 with 0.01%." In fact $0.01 = 1\%$, but $0.01\% = \frac{0.01}{100} = 0.0001$.
- Finding a percent from part and whole (p. 22): "first divide the part by the whole to get the decimal equivalent, then multiply the result by 100. The percent is that number followed by the word 'percent' or the % symbol."
  - $\frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \frac{13}{20} = 0.65 = 65\%$
  - "What percent of 150 is 12.9?" gives $\frac{12.9}{150} = 0.086 = 8.6\%$
- Finding the part (pp. 22–23): "multiply the *whole* by the decimal equivalent of the percent or set up a proportion."
  - 30% of 350: $(350)(0.3) = 105$.
  - The same by proportion: $\frac{x}{350} = \frac{30}{100}$ gives $x = \frac{(30)(350)}{100} = 105$.
- Finding the whole (pp. 23–24): use the decimal equivalent or a proportion.
  - "15 is 60% of what number?" gives $0.6z = 15$, so $z = \frac{15}{0.6} = 25$.
  - The same by proportion: $\frac{15}{z} = \frac{60}{100}$, so $60z = (15)(100)$ and $z = \frac{1{,}500}{60} = 25$.
- Percents greater than 100% (p. 24): "it is not necessary that the part be less than the whole."
  - 15 is 300% of 5, since $\frac{15}{5} = \frac{300}{100}$.
  - 250% of 16 is 40, since $\left(\frac{250}{100}\right)(16) = (2.5)(16) = 40$.
  - The decimal equivalent of 300% is 3.0, and of 250% is 2.5.
- Percent increase (p. 25):
  $$\frac{\text{amount of increase}}{\text{base}} = \frac{750 - 600}{600} = \frac{150}{600} = \frac{25}{100} = 25\%$$
  Sometimes written $\left(\frac{750-600}{600}\right)(100\%) = \left(\frac{150}{600}\right)(100\%) = 25\%$.
- Doubling (p. 25): "If a quantity doubles in size, then the percent increase is 100%." Example: $\frac{300-150}{150} = \frac{150}{150} = 100\%$.
- Percent decrease (p. 25):
  $$\frac{\text{amount of decrease}}{\text{base}} = \frac{500 - 400}{500} = \frac{100}{500} = \frac{20}{100} = 20\%$$
- Which number is the base (p. 25): "When computing a percent *increase*, the base is the *smaller* number. When computing a percent *decrease*, the base is the *larger* number. In either case, the base is the initial number, before the change."
- Applying an increase (pp. 25–26), Ex. 1.7.15, a 12% increase on \$1,300:
  - $(0.12)(\$1{,}300) = \$156$, and $\$1{,}300 + \$156 = \$1{,}456$.
  - Equivalently $100\% + 12\% = 112\%$, so $(\$1{,}300)(1.12) = \$1{,}456$.
- Successive percent changes (p. 26): "A quantity may have several successive percent changes, where the base of each successive change is the result of the preceding percent change."
  - Ex. 1.7.16 (pp. 26–27): an 8% decrease followed by a 6% increase gives $(1.06)(0.92n) = 0.9752n$.
  - 97.52% is 2.48% less than 100%, so the overall change is "a 2.48% decrease".
  - The decrease uses $(100 - 8)\% = 92\%$ and the increase uses $(100 + 6)\% = 106\%$.

### Notation / conventions
- ETS writes percent change as "the percent increase is 25%" and "the quantity decreased by 20%". A net change is phrased as "a 2.48% decrease" (p. 27).
- "$x$% less than" and "$x$% greater than" refer to the earlier quantity as the base. Example: "8% less than the number … on September 1, 2012" gives $0.92n$ (p. 26).
- Percent change is defined for a change "from an initial positive amount to another positive amount" (p. 24).
- In proportions, ETS writes $\frac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \frac{p}{100}$ (pp. 23–24).

### Exercise styles
Exercises 9–12 (p. 30):
- finding a percent of a number, including percents over 100% and under 1%;
- finding the whole given a part and a percent;
- finding what percent one number is of another;
- percent increase and percent decrease from given before and after values;
- a successive increase followed by a decrease applied to a price.

### Gotchas
- 0.01 is not the same as 0.01%. The first is 1%, the second is 0.0001 (p. 22).
- The base is always the initial value, before the change (p. 25).
- Successive percent changes do not add. An 8% decrease then a 6% increase is a net 2.48% decrease, not a 2% decrease (pp. 26–27). Likewise, Exercise 12 (+20% then −25% on \$40) gives \$36, not \$38 (p. 34).
- A percent can exceed 100% when the part is larger than the base (p. 24).
- Doubling is a 100% increase, not a 200% increase (p. 25).

## Relevant Math Conventions

From *Math Conventions for the GRE General Test* (`conventions.pdf`). Page numbers are printed page numbers, which equal PDF page numbers.

### Numbers and Quantities (pp. 4–5)
1. **Real numbers only** (p. 4): "All numbers used in the test questions are real numbers. In particular, integers and both rational and irrational numbers are to be considered, but imaginary numbers are not. … Also, all quantities are real numbers, although quantities may involve units of measurement."
2. **Base 10 and digit grouping** (p. 4): "Numbers are expressed in base 10 unless otherwise noted, using the 10 digits 0 through 9 and a period to the right of the ones digit, or units digit, for the decimal point. Also, in numbers that are 1,000 or greater, commas are used to separate groups of three digits to the left of the decimal point."
3. **Number of digits** (p. 4): when a positive integer is described by its number of digits, "the digits that are counted include the ones digit and all the digits further to the left, where the leftmost digit is not 0." So 5,000 is a four-digit integer, but 031 is not a three-digit integer.
4. **Named numbers** (p. 4):
   - **one billion** means $1{,}000{,}000{,}000$, or $10^9$ (not $10^{12}$, as in some countries).
   - **one dozen** means 12.
   - $\pi$ is the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter and is approximately 3.14.
5. **Rounding** (p. 4): "When a positive number is to be rounded to a certain decimal place and the number is halfway between the two nearest possibilities, the number should be rounded to the greater possibility." So 23.5 rounds to 24, and 123.985 rounded to the nearest 0.01 is 123.99. "When the number to be rounded is negative, the number should be rounded to the lesser possibility." So $-36.5$ rounds to $-37$. In other words, halves round away from zero in both cases.
6. **Repeating decimals** (p. 4): these "are sometimes written with a bar over the digits that repeat", as in $\frac{25}{12} = 2.08\overline{3}$ and $\frac{1}{7} = 0.\overline{142857}$.
7. **Factor, divisor, multiple, divisible** (pp. 4–5):
   - "If $r$, $s$, and $t$ are integers and $rs = t$, then $r$ and $s$ are factors, or divisors, of $t$; also, $t$ is a multiple of $r$ (and of $s$) and $t$ is divisible by $r$ (and by $s$)."
   - "The factors of an integer include positive and negative integers." Examples: $-7$ is a factor of 35; 8 is a factor of $-40$; 4 has six factors, $-4, -2, -1, 1, 2, 4$.
   - "The terms factor, divisor, and divisible are used only when $r$, $s$, and $t$ are integers. However, the term multiple can be used with any real numbers $s$ and $t$ provided that $r$ is an integer." Examples: 1.2 is a multiple of 0.4, and $-2\pi$ is a multiple of $\pi$.
8. **LCM and GCD** (p. 5): "The least common multiple of two nonzero integers $a$ and $b$ is the least positive integer that is a multiple of both $a$ and $b$. The greatest common divisor (or greatest common factor) of $a$ and $b$ is the greatest positive integer that is a divisor of both $a$ and $b$."
9. **Division with remainder** (p. 5): "If an integer $n$ is divided by a nonzero integer $d$ resulting in a quotient $q$ with remainder $r$, then $n = qd + r$, where $0 \le r < |d|$. Furthermore, $r = 0$ if and only if $n$ is a multiple of $d$."
   - 20 ÷ 7 gives quotient 2, remainder 6.
   - 21 ÷ 7 gives quotient 3, remainder 0.
   - $-17 \div 7$ gives quotient $-3$, remainder 4.
   - (note, not ETS: this convention allows a negative divisor, with the bound $|d|$. The Math Review p. 5 states the procedure only for a positive divisor.)
10. **Prime and composite** (p. 5): "A prime number is an integer greater than 1 that has only two positive divisors: 1 and itself. The first five prime numbers are 2, 3, 5, 7, and 11. A composite number is an integer greater than 1 that is not a prime number. The first five composite numbers are 4, 6, 8, 9, and 10."
11. **Odd and even** (p. 5): "Odd and even integers are not necessarily positive." For example, $-7$ is odd, and $-18$ and 0 are even.
12. **Zero** (p. 5): "The integer 0 is neither positive nor negative."

### Mathematical Expressions, Symbols, and Variables (pp. 5–7)
1. **Letters** (p. 5): italic letters like $x$ denote numbers, constants, and variables. Letters also label objects (line $\ell$, point $P$, function $f$, set $S$, list $T$, event $E$, random variable $X$, Brand $X$, City $Y$, Company $Z$). "The meaning of a letter is determined by the context."
2. **Possible values** (pp. 5–6): "When numbers, constants, or variables are given, their possible values are all real numbers unless otherwise restricted." Examples of restrictions:
   - "$n$ is a nonzero integer";
   - $1 \le x < \pi$;
   - "$T$ is the tens digit of a two-digit positive integer, so $T$ is an integer from 1 to 9."
3. **Standard symbols** (p. 6):
   - The arithmetic symbols are $+$, $-$, $\times$ and $\div$.
   - "multiplication is usually denoted by juxtaposition, often with parentheses", e.g. $2y$ and $(3)(4.5)$.
   - "division is usually denoted with a horizontal fraction bar", e.g. $\frac{w}{3}$.
   - Mixed numbers are used, e.g. $4\frac{3}{8} = \frac{35}{8}$ and $-10\frac{1}{2} = -\frac{21}{2}$.
   - Exponents are used, e.g. $2^{10} = 1{,}024$, $10^{-2} = \frac{1}{100}$, and $x^0 = 1$ for all nonzero numbers $x$.
4. **Order of operations** (p. 6): "parentheses, exponentiation, negation, multiplication and division (from left to right), addition and subtraction (from left to right)."
   - $1 + 2 \times 4 = 9$.
   - $-3^2$ means "the negative of '3 squared'" because exponentiation takes precedence over negation, so $-3^2 = -9$.
   - But $(-3)^2 = 9$, because parentheses take precedence over exponentiation.
5. **Standard symbols and their meanings** (pp. 6–7). The arithmetic-relevant ones are:

   | Symbol | Meaning |
   |---|---|
   | $x \le y$ | $x$ is less than or equal to $y$ |
   | $x \ne y$ | $x$ is not equal to $y$ |
   | $x \approx y$ | $x$ is approximately equal to $y$ |
   | $\lvert x\rvert$ | the absolute value of $x$ |
   | $\sqrt{x}$ | the nonnegative square root of $x$, where $x \ge 0$ |
   | $-\sqrt{x}$ | the negative square root of $x$, where $x > 0$ |
   | $n!$ | $n$ factorial, the product of all positive integers less than or equal to $n$, where $n$ is any positive integer; as a special definition, $0! = 1$ |

   The table also lists the geometry symbols $k \parallel m$, $k \perp m$ and $\angle B$, which are not arithmetic.
6. **Undefined expressions** (p. 7): "Because all numbers are assumed to be real, some expressions are not defined." Examples:
   - $\frac{x}{0}$ is not defined for every number $x$;
   - $\sqrt{x}$ is not defined if $x < 0$;
   - $0^0$ is not defined.
7. **Special operations** (p. 7): "Sometimes special symbols or notation are introduced in a question." Examples:
   - $r \mathbin{\Diamond} s = \frac{rs}{1 + r^2}$ for all integers $r$ and $s$;
   - $\sim x = -\frac{1}{x}$ for all nonzero $x$.
8. **Juxtaposition of digits** (p. 7): "Sometimes juxtaposition of letters does *not* denote multiplication, as in 'consider a three-digit positive integer denoted by $BCD$, where $B$, $C$, and $D$ are digits.' Whether or not juxtaposition of letters denotes multiplication depends on the context in which the juxtaposition occurs."

### Coordinate Systems (p. 11), as it applies to number lines
- Number lines are drawn to scale, so you can read, estimate or compare quantities on them by sight or by measurement.
- On a horizontal number line the positive direction is to the right, and on a vertical one it is upward, unless otherwise noted.
- "distances in a coordinate system are nonnegative."
- Intermediate tick marks are evenly spaced unless otherwise noted (p. 12).

### Sets, Lists, and Sequences (pp. 12–13)
- **Sets** (p. 12): "When the elements of a set are given, repetitions are *not* counted as additional elements and the order of the elements is *not* relevant." Examples are the set of integers (infinite) and $\{2, 4, 6, 8\}$.
- **Lists** (p. 13): "When the elements of a list are given, repetitions *are* counted as additional elements and the order of the elements *is* relevant." Example: the list 3, 1, 2, 3, 3 contains five numbers.
- **Sequences** (p. 13): ellipsis dots at the end of a list of terms mean there is no last term. Example: $-2, -4, -6, -8, -10, \ldots$ are consecutive even negative integers.
- **Consecutive integers, inclusive** (p. 13): "the integers from 0 to 9, inclusive" refers to 10 integers, "with or without 'inclusive' at the end." So "during the years from 1985 to 2005" refers to 21 years.

### Data and Statistics (p. 13), as it applies to ratios
- "Without the qualification of 'arithmetic mean,' **average** can refer to a rate or the ratio of one quantity to another, as in 'average number of miles per hour' or 'average weight per truckload.'"

### Graphical Representations of Data (p. 16), as it applies to percent
- "a circle graph represents 100 percent of the data indicated in the graph's title, and the areas of the individual sectors are proportional to the percents they represent."

### Miscellaneous Guidelines (pp. 17–18)
1. **Exact numbers** (p. 17): "Numbers given in a question are to be used as exact numbers, even though in some real-life settings they are likely to have been rounded." For example, "30 percent" means exactly 30, not 29 or 30.1.
2. **Counts are totals** (p. 17): "An integer that is given as the number of certain objects … is to be taken as the total number of such objects." For example, 50 marbles with 23 red means the other 27 are not red. "Fractions and percents are understood in a similar way, so 'one-fifth, or 20 percent, of the 50 marbles in the bag are green' means that 10 marbles in the bag are green and 40 marbles are not green."
3. **Approximate answers** (p. 17): when a multiple-choice question asks for an approximate quantity without stating the degree of approximation, the correct answer is "the choice that is closest in value to the quantity that can be computed from the information given."
4. **Difference** (p. 17): "Unless otherwise indicated, the phrase 'difference between two quantities' is assumed to mean 'positive difference,' that is, the greater quantity minus the lesser quantity." In other words, it is the absolute value of the difference.
5. **Profit** (p. 17): profit means **gross profit**, "the sales revenue minus the cost of production or acquisition," unless other amounts are explicitly given.
6. **Everyday terms** (p. 17): months, years and similar terms have their common meaning.
7. **Positive in real-life contexts** (pp. 17–18): when a variable represents a number of existing objects or a monetary amount, "the context implies that the variable is greater than 0 unless otherwise noted." Example: "Jane sold $x$ rugs and deposited her profit of $y$ dollars" implies $x, y > 0$.
8. **Units** (p. 18): any units may be used. If converting units is needed, the relationship is given unless it is a common one, such as minutes and hours, dollars and cents, or centimeters and meters.
9. **Unneeded information** (p. 18): a question may contain information that is not needed.
10. **No unwarranted assumptions** (p. 18): for example, ignore time zones or daylight saving unless they are mentioned, and do not consider sales tax unless it is explicitly mentioned.
11. **Expressions inside phrases** (p. 18): "each mathematical expression should be interpreted *separately* from the words before it is interpreted *along with* the words." For example, with $n$ an integer, "the sum of the first two consecutive integers greater than $n + 6$" means $(n+7) + (n+8)$, not $(n+1) + (n+2) + 6$. However, "for all $x \ge 0$" is shorthand for "for all numbers $x$ such that $x \ge 0$."
